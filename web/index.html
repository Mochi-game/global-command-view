            What this is
            
      
            
        A live picture of the world built only from public feeds: aircraft, ships,
        satellites, submarine cables, road cameras, earthquakes. Everything is
        labelled with where it came from, and everything that is a guess says so.
      
            

      
            1 · Moving around
            
      
              
        
            • Drag to turn the globe, scroll to zoom.
              • 
        
            • Middle-drag, or Ctrl-drag, to tilt the camera.
              • 
        
            • Jump to has a search box above its presets. It takes whatever
          you have: a coordinate, an ICAO or IATA code like ESSA or
          LAX, an airport name, or a town. Coordinates are read in
          whatever form they are written — decimal, degrees and decimal
          minutes as a kneeboard gives them, or degrees, minutes and seconds
          — with the hemisphere letter before or after, and with or without
          the degree marks. Underneath it says what it decided your query was,
          because a coordinate read here and a name looked up in somebody's
          gazetteer are different kinds of answer.
              • 
        
            • Jump to in the left panel has preset views. Click a section
          heading to fold it away when the panel gets crowded.
              • 
      
            

      
            2 · Layers
            
      
            
        Each row in Layers is a feed with a live count. Click to switch it
        off. Most layers only load what is in view, so panning somewhere new
        fetches that area rather than the whole planet.
      
            
      
            Everything starts off except names and borders and the public
        cameras, and a layer that is off is not fetched at all — switching
        it on is what loads it. That keeps the start quiet and the bill small.
            

      
            Ground change is the group worth knowing about. Radar
        backscatter, ground disturbance and surface water all come from NASA's
        OPERA products at about 30 m, and the radar ones are the only layers
        here that see through cloud and darkness — which is usually exactly
        when something is happening. They lag by a couple of days, because a
        swath has to be flown and processed, and each one prints its own date in
        the feed. This is imagery of something that happened, not a live view.
            

      
            Data centres & dams are queried live from OpenStreetMap for
        whatever is on screen, so they need you to zoom in; at world scale the
        feed says so rather than drawing an empty map that reads as
        “there are no dams”. Most dams in OpenStreetMap have no name
        and the card says unnamed rather than guessing one.
            

      
            Rocket launches are a schedule, not a fact. The status is printed
        untranslated — Go, TBD, Hold — and anything not Go is drawn
        dim, because half of spaceflight slips to the right.
            

      
            
        To isolate one thing — say police helicopters — switch off the layers
        around it. Police & state air and Capital ships are
        separate layers exactly for that.
      
            

      
            Sweden: three layers off one key
            
      
            
        A Sweden group sits at the bottom of the layer list. Two of its
        three layers come from the Trafikverket key that was already needed for
        the Swedish road cameras — the same key, no new account. The third
        needs no key at all.
      
            
      
              
        
            • Swedish road disruption — roadworks, traffic notices and
          ferry information across the state road network, about 1 200 of them at
          any time. Coloured by Trafikverket’s own severity: grey for no
          impact through to red for mycket stor påverkan. Refreshed
          every three minutes. A handful of entries describe a stretch of road
          rather than a spot; those have no single point to draw and the feed log
          says how many were left out rather than quietly rounding them away.
              • 
        
            • Swedish trains — where trains report themselves to be,
          refreshed every thirty seconds. This is a position, not a timetable:
          nothing here says whether a train is late. Trafikverket keep a position
          after a train has finished with it, so anything older than fifteen
          minutes is dropped rather than drawn as a train standing still, and the
          count of what was dropped is printed.
              • 
        
            • SMHI warnings — weather, water and fire warnings in
          force, drawn as the areas they are. A wind warning covers a
          coastline, not a spot on it, so there is no pin: the middle of the
          shape is not more warned than the edge. Yellow, orange and red are
          SMHI’s own scale and are kept unchanged; Meddelande is the
          level below yellow and is information rather than a warning, drawn
          faint. No key, no account, CC BY 4.0.
              • 
      
            
      
            
        All three are Sweden only, and the first two are the state road and
        rail network only. A municipal street closure is not in them, and neither
        is a train that is not reporting. Fly to Denmark and they are empty
        — correctly, not because anything broke.
      
            

      
            3 · Detection mode
            
      
            
        DETECTION ON puts a reticle and a designator on contacts:
        SAT-37218, MIL-GAF942, POL-BPO317,
        SEA-265068000. The Density slider sets how many are
        labelled. The screen is divided into cells and each cell surrenders its
        most central contact, so labels spread out instead of piling up.
      
            

      
            4 · Optics
            
      
              
        
            • OPS — dark cartographic base, best for reading positions.
              • 
        
            • SATELLITE — aerial imagery.
              • 
        
            • THERMAL, NIGHT VIS, FLIR — sensor emulations.
              • 
        
            • CRT — a real post-process pass: barrel distortion, scan lines,
          chromatic separation.
              • 
        
            • SENTINEL 10M — sharp, and not dated. It is a
          cloudless mosaic composited from a year of passes, so it has no clouds,
          no smoke, no ships and no flood in it. A basemap, not a look.
              • 
        
            • Any style from your own Copernicus instance — the
          opposite trade. One acquisition at 10 m, with whatever was in the air
          still in it, following the imagery-day slider. Needs a free account;
          the Setup tab has the steps and the two limits worth knowing.
              • 
      
            

      
            5 · Clicking things
            
      
            
        Any contact opens a card. Aircraft carry a photograph of the airframe and,
        when the flight is scheduled, the route drawn on the globe — solid from the
        origin to where it is now, dashed onward. Ships carry a photograph from
        Wikimedia Commons where one exists. Cameras carry their live image; click
        the image for a full-screen viewer with scroll-to-zoom.
      
            
      
            Click the ISS in the satellite layer and the card lists its
        next passes over wherever the camera is pointed — start time, how
        high it climbs, how long it lasts and how close it gets. Worked out from
        the same orbit the dot on screen is drawn from, so the two agree by
        construction. Nothing below ten degrees is listed: lower than that it is
        behind whatever is on your horizon, and printing it would be a promise
        the sky does not keep.
            

      
            A calibrated camera also gets a cone on the ground showing what
        it can see. Only calibrated ones: the feeds carry a position and nothing
        else, so for any other camera the direction would be invented. Set it
        with PROJECT ONTO GROUND and the calibration sliders, and the cone
        appears as you save.
            

      
            
        Moving contacts get a FOLLOW button: the camera locks on and stays
        locked while you orbit around it. Cameras get PROJECT ONTO GROUND,
        which paints the live frame onto the map — aim it with the calibration
        sliders, since no camera feed publishes which way it points.
      
            

      
            6 · Going down to the ground
            
      
              
        
            • DROP TO GROUND dives to roughly 300 m.
              • 
        
            • STAND HERE then a click puts you on the spot at eye height.
          Drag turns your head, the wheel is a zoom lens, Esc lifts you out.
              • 
        
            • OSM buildings are extruded footprints with real heights — grey
          boxes, not photogrammetry.
              • 
        
            • Street-level photos are the little arrows on the ground, real
          photographs from KartaView. Click one to open it.
              • 
      
            

      
            7 · Measuring and marking
            
      
            
        MEASURE DISTANCE turns clicks into legs with a running total,
        measured on the ellipsoid. It is how you tell a 170 m submarine from a
        110 m one. MARK saves the whole camera — position, height, heading
        and pitch — under a name, so you return to the view and not just the spot.
        Marks are kept in a file on the server and survive everything.
      
            

      
            8 · Reading the top bar
            
      
              
        
            • VIS / SRC / DENS — contacts labelled, contacts visible,
          label density, and how long a detection pass took.
              • 
        
            • EYE — camera height.
              • 
        
            • IMG — when the satellite image under you was taken,
          with its resolution. Hover for the provider and age; it turns warm past
          two years. The map always looks live and never is.
              • 
      
            

      
            What is real and what is not
            
      
              
        
            • Real observations: aircraft, ships on AIS, cameras, earthquakes,
          cable routes, satellite orbits.
              • 
        
            • Modelled: nothing. A simulated traffic layer was removed in
          0.49 — it was labelled honestly, but a moving dot that is not a car is
          decoration, and there is no feed of where cars actually are.
              • 
        
            • Estimated: capital ships. Carriers do not broadcast; those are
          last-reported operating areas from the USNI weekly tracker, drawn with a
          ring showing the uncertainty and dated on the card.
              • 
        
            • Not tracked at all: submarines. The layer marks where they are
          based. Fly there, read the imagery date, and measure what is at the pier.
              • 
      
            
    
            

    
            
      
            
        The app works with no accounts at all. Each key below adds coverage that
        the free-for-all sources do not reach. Paste one in and press Save —
        it is written to keys.json beside the app and takes effect
        immediately, no restart.
      
            
      
            
        Keys are stored in plain text on this machine and are never sent anywhere
        except to the service they belong to. The page never shows a key back to
        you once saved — only whether one is set.
      
            
      
            

    
            

    
            
      
            
        Every feed this app draws from, and what its licence allows. The short
        version: giving this away is fine, selling it is not — several
        sources permit non-commercial use only.
      
            
      
            
        A voluntary tip is not a sale. A payment somebody has to make to
        get the app or unlock a feature is, whatever it is called — and that
        breaks the terms below. Keep every feature free for everyone and the
        coffee stays a thank-you.
      
            
      
            
    
            

    
            
      
            
        Every feed here is free, and free comes with edges. This is what the
        edges look like from the inside, so that a layer reading zero can be
        told apart from a layer that is broken.
      
            

      
            When a count says zero
            
      
              
        
            • Air traffic at global zoom. OpenSky is the only source that
          answers for the whole planet, and its free tier is a daily credit
          allowance. When it is spent the server logs
          opensky quota spent, falling back to adsb.fi and switches. The
          fallback can only be asked in circles around wherever you are looking
          — eight of 250 nm — which covers almost nothing from 24 000 km up.
          Zoom in and the aircraft come back: the same moment a global
          view showed 0, Europe showed 1 468. The quota resets by itself.
              • 
        
            • Anything, when you are zoomed in. Contacts outside the view
          are hidden on purpose — thirty thousand of them at once is what made
          the app stutter. The counts are what is in frame, not what exists.
              • 
        
            • Vessels outside the Baltic. Digitraffic is Finnish and covers
          the Baltic properly. Worldwide AIS comes from aisstream, which has been
          connected and silent for a long stretch — their own issue tracker says
          so. The feed log states which source answered.
              • 
      
            

      
            What each key costs, and what runs out
            
      
              
        
            • Cesium ion — terrain and 3D buildings. Community tier is free
          and licensed for personal, non-commercial use. No metering that
          matters here.
              • 
        
            • OpenAQ — air quality. Free key, generous limits.
          Without it the layer says so in the feed rather than drawing
          nothing.
              • 
        
            • Global Fishing Watch — fishing, encounters and
          transponder gaps. Free token, but granted by hand rather than
          instantly, so expect a wait.
              • 
        
            • TomTom — measured traffic flow. Free tier. Restrict the
          key by referrer the way the Google one is: the browser fetches the
          tiles directly, so the key reaches the page.
              • 
        
            • NASA OPERA, Launch Library, OpenStreetMap — no account
          at all. Nothing to run out.
              • 
        
            • Google photoreal 3D — billed per root tile request,
          which is one per session however far you fly afterwards.
          1 000 free a month. The switch asks for exactly one, the first
          time it goes on.
              
          Lost to the EEA terms. Google does not serve satellite
          tiles or photorealistic 3D tiles to projects billed to an address in
          the European Economic Area, and answers 403. A project
          created on or after 8 July 2025 with EEA billing is not
          eligible for the exemption at all. One created before that date keeps
          it only while it is not materially modified — and
          enabling another Maps Platform service in the console counts, Street
          View among them. Google document no way back from either. Note the
          date that matters is the Cloud project’s, not this
          app’s. World terrain and worldwide 3D buildings from Cesium ion
          are unaffected.
              • 
        
            • Google Street View — billed per image.
          10 000 free a month. The metadata check that decides whether a
          panorama exists at all is free and unlimited, so it is always asked
          first: a spot with no coverage costs nothing. The view steps in 45°
          rather than following the mouse, and a spot already visited is reused
          rather than bought twice.
              • 
        
            • Windy webcams — the free tier stops paging at about 1 050 of
          their 70 000, and is licensed for link-and-embed only.
              • 
        
            • OpenSky — daily credits, as above.
              • 
        
            • Trafikverket, Digitraffic, aisstream — free, no meaningful
          cap.
              • 
      
            
      
            
        Google spend in the panel shows what is left rather than what is
        spent, amber at 80 per cent and red at the cap. It counts what this app
        asked for; the console linked beneath it is the authority.
      
            

      
            Ceilings you will notice
            
      
              
        
            • NASA imagery stops at 300 m a pixel. FIRE IR and SMOKE come
          from GIBS, which publishes to zoom level 9 — one screen pixel per
          image pixel at about 340 km up. Below roughly 140 km the app hands the
          globe to the sharper commercial mosaic and says so, then gives the
          false colour back above 280 km. In commercial-safe mode it refuses to
          hand over, because nothing sharper here has an unambiguous licence.
              • 
        
            • Esri imagery stops at level 19, about 30 cm. Level 20 exists
          in a few places and returns grey elsewhere, so it is not used.
              • 
        
            • Street View is 640 × 640 and no larger. Asking for 2048
          returns 640 without mentioning it.
              • 
        
            • Overpass buildings come from public mirrors with very few
          slots. When they all refuse, the app stops asking for five minutes
          rather than holding the server hostage, and says so.
              • 
        
            • Place names come from Nominatim at one request a second, by
          their terms. A briefing gives it six seconds and then goes out with
          some rows unlabelled — a missing place beats a briefing that never
          arrives.
              • 
      
            

      
            What a number means before you say it aloud
            
      
              
        
            • Thermal detections are not wildfires. A gas flare, a volcano,
          a steel works and a farmer burning stubble all read the same from
          orbit, and the bulk feed has no field that separates them. The layer is
          called Thermal for that reason. Fire fronts in the briefing are
          clustered and ranked by total power, so one hot pixel cannot top the
          list.
              • 
        
            • “No international alert” is not “nobody knows”. GDACS alerts
          above a threshold in hectares. A local fire service almost certainly
          knows about a fire GDACS has never heard of.
              • 
        
            • Outbreak markers sit on country centroids, because that is the
          resolution WHO publishes at. The dot is not where the cases are. The
          date is when WHO published, never when the outbreak ended.
              • 
        
            • Aviation radio is mostly out of reach, and the app says which
          part. Tower and approach are VHF around 118–137 MHz; the
          KiwiSDR network stops at 30 MHz, so those receivers physically cannot
          hear a control tower. What they can hear is oceanic HF —
          Shanwick and Gander working aircraft across the Atlantic, and the VOLMET
          weather loops — and the tuning presets cover it. For VHF the
          airport card links to LiveATC, who carry it.
              • 
        
            • Listening is legal; it is the only part that is. In the United
          States, receiving unencrypted radio is lawful under federal law —
          the wiretap statute exempts communications readily accessible to the
          general public, which is what an unencrypted transmission is. Several
          states restrict using a scanner in a vehicle or while committing
          an offence, so the state matters. Recording, rebroadcasting and
          publishing are separate questions from listening, and none of this is
          legal advice.
              • 
        
            • The disease layer only sees international escalation. WHO
          publishes Disease Outbreak News when something crosses a threshold of
          international concern. An outbreak a national agency is handling never
          reaches it — in August 2026 there were 27 measles cases across nine
          Swedish regions, traced to one festival, and nothing about it appears on
          this globe. An empty map of Europe is not an empty Europe. There
          is no open, keyless, real-time feed of national outbreaks to add; for
          those, ask the country's own agency, or ECDC for Europe.
              • 
        
            • A nearby alert may be a different event. When a fire cluster
          matches an alert 149 km away, the distance is shown rather than
          smoothed over.
              • 
        
            • Quakes are ranked by magnitude across seven days, so a large
          old one can outrank a fresh smaller one. Every row carries its age.
              • 
        
            • Carriers are estimates from a weekly published tracker, drawn
          with an uncertainty ring and dated. Submarines are not tracked at all —
          that layer marks bases.
              • 
      
            

      
            What is deliberately not here
            
      
            
        This app was built alongside a much larger open-source project in the
        same field, and most of what that project reads openly is now read here
        too — with our own client code, so this stays MIT. These are the
        ones that did not make it, and why. A gap with a reason is worth more
        than a feature that half works.
            
      
              
        
            • Telegram channel scraping. Technically simple and left out on
          purpose: it means republishing war footage from channels with agendas,
          geoparsed by guesswork onto a map. The honesty this app is built
          around does not survive that.
              • 
        
            • Shodan device search. Deliberately not drawn as a map. A
          chart of individually located exposed cameras and control systems, on
          a public stream, is a targeting aid rather than a picture of the
          world. The keyless half — open ports and known vulnerabilities
          for one address you type in — is in the Recon panel already, and
          a per-country count would be the honest way to draw the rest.
              • 
        
            • Copernicus EGMS millimetre subsidence. Ground sinking a few
          millimetres a year, which is how you see a mine or an aquifer give
          way before anything is visible. It wants an account and a different
          kind of tiling than anything here does. A real gap, not a principle.
              • 
        
            • Finnish train positions. Digitraffic's train endpoint answers
          406 to every Accept header tried, while the same host's ships and
          cameras work. Absent rather than broken.
              • 
        
            • Scanner audio. The OpenMHZ system list loads and the
          layer draws; playing the recorded calls in-page is what does not, and
          each system's own page is one click away on the card.
              • 
      
            

      
            If something looks wrong
            
      
              
        
            • Read the feed log at the bottom of the panel. Every failure
          writes a line there in plain words, including which source answered and
          why one refused.
              • 
        
            • Check the version chip beside the title. If it reads
          v— the app did not finish starting, and nothing on screen
          can be trusted.
              • 
        
            • Run the check. Check Global Command View in the
          project folder reads the files, calls every feed and says what broke.
          A green result means it boots and every source answers — not that the
          picture is right. That still needs your eyes.
              • 
        
            • Do not minimise the window while recording. Browsers throttle
          hidden windows, and the globe then stops streaming tiles. Keep it
          visible behind OBS, or on a second screen.
              • 
      
            

      
            If it feels heavy
            
      
            
        The globe redraws continuously by default — sixty times a second
        whether or not anything has changed. On a desktop with its own graphics
        card that costs nothing you would notice. On a laptop with integrated
        graphics it is a loud fan and a flat battery, often while nothing on
        screen is moving at all.
      
            
      
            
        Performance mode, under Broadcast, stops that. The main saving is
        not lower quality: it is not drawing what has not changed. Anything that
        moves under its own steam — aircraft, vessels, satellites, a
        followed contact — still animates, at twenty frames a second rather
        than sixty. Fog, atmosphere and 3D buildings come off, and terrain is
        asked for at roughly half the detail.
      
            
      
            
        Nothing in it changes what the data says. It changes how often the
        picture is painted, and how much scenery is painted with it.
      
            
      
            
        The app also times its own frames. If it is drawing below about thirty a
        second once the tiles have settled, it says so in the feed log, once, and
        points at the switch. It will not ask twice.
      
            
    
            

    
            
      
            The argument
            
      
            An open-source globe is easy to make impressive and hard to make honest.
        Anything at all can be drawn as a moving dot. The question nobody asks of
        a dashboard is the only one that matters: is that dot a measurement, a
        guess, or a decoration?
            

      
            This app answers it everywhere it can — in the layer note, in the
        feed log, and on the card when you click a thing. Those answers use four
        words, and they mean specific things.
            

      
            The four kinds of thing on this globe
            
      
              
        
            • Measured. A sensor or a transponder reported it and the app is
          passing it on. An aircraft broadcasting ADS-B, a ship on AIS, a
          seismograph, a radar swath. The position is as good as the instrument.
              • 
        
            • Inferred. A model turned measurements into a conclusion.
          Satellite positions propagated from orbital elements, a vessel's
          behaviour read out of its track, internet outages deduced from three
          methods agreeing. Right most of the time, and wrong in ways that look
          just as confident.
              • 
        
            • An area, not a point. Known to be somewhere in a region and
          drawn at its centre with a ring the size of the region. Carriers, whose
          weekly tracker names a sea. Outbreak reports that name a country. The
          ring is the honest part — without it the dot would be a lie.
              • 
        
            • Hand-entered. Somebody read it and typed it in, with a source
          link. No feed saw it. Own entries, and the catalogue layers where a
          curator, not a sensor, decided the thing was worth listing.
              • 
      
            

      
            Things this deliberately does not do
            
      
            It does not smooth a gap into a line, invent a heading for a camera that
        never reported one, or animate a vehicle that no feed can see. Simulated
        traffic was built here once and taken out the next day, because a moving
        dot that is not a car is worse than no dot at all: it looks like
        information.
            
      
            When a source is silent the feed says which one and why. A count of
        zero means the feed answered and had nothing — it never means
        the app gave up quietly.
            

      
            Where it runs
            
      
            On your machine. One Python file serving a browser page, no account
        needed for most of it, no telemetry, and no server but the one on your
        own disk. What leaves your computer are requests to the public feeds
        listed under SOURCES & LICENCES, and nothing else.
            

      
            Moving it to another computer
            
      
            
        Everything that is yours lives in four files inside the app
        folder. There is no .env, no profile, and nothing stored
        anywhere but on your own disk, so moving the app is copying files.
      
            
      
            
        Install it on the new machine the ordinary way — unpack the
        release, check Python is there — then copy these across into the
        same places, and start it:
      
            
      
              
        
            • keys.json — your API keys. This one on its own
          is enough to be running again. If you only copy one file, copy
          this.
              • 
        
            • data/marks.json — the views you have marked and
          named.
              • 
        
            • data/manual_events.json — anything you entered by
          hand into the briefing.
              • 
        
            • data/usage.json — the Google spend counters.
              • 
      
            
      
            
        Keys restricted by referrer keep working without being touched. The
        restriction is to 127.0.0.1:8820, and that is localhost on
        the new machine as much as on the old one.
      
            
      
            
        One honest catch, on that last file. usage.json counts
        locally, but your Google quota belongs to the project, not to the
        computer. Run this on two machines with the same key and each one counts
        only its own calls, so both bars read low while the real total is the sum
        of them. The Google console is the authority, which the spend panel says
        there too. If the second machine is a replacement rather than an
        addition, copy the file and the count stays honest.
