Open-source situational awareness

Every dot on this globe tells you where it came from.

Twelve thousand aircraft, twenty-four thousand ships, four thousand public cameras, radar that sees through cloud, and the radio you can actually listen to — on one globe. None of it is simulated.

The last line is the point. It does not say ten outbreaks. It says fifteen of them could not be put anywhere, so they are not on the map.

The argument

Most maps of the world are confident. This one is checkable.

An OSINT globe is easy to make impressive and hard to make honest. Anything can be drawn as a moving dot. The question nobody asks of a dashboard is the only one that matters: is that dot a measurement, a guess, or a decoration?

Global Command View answers it on every layer, in the feed log, and on every card you open. A carrier is drawn at the centre of the sea it was last reported in, inside a ring the size of that sea — because a weekly tracker says an area, never a position. A thermal detection is called a thermal detection, not a wildfire, because the satellite measured heat and nothing more. A vessel that goes dark gets a sentence saying transponders fail for dull reasons, so the gap is a question and not an accusation.

A missing pin is recoverable. A confidently wrong one is not.

That discipline is why simulated traffic was removed after a day, and why there are no animated cars: a moving dot that is not a car is worse than no dot, because it looks like information.

What is on it

Thirty-seven layers, each one labelled by what kind of truth it is.

Everything starts switched off except names, borders and the public cameras — a layer that is off is not fetched at all. Turn on what you want and the panel tells you how many of each it found, and where it came from.

Measured

A sensor or transponder reported it.

Inferred

Derived from measurements by a model.

Area, not point

Known to be somewhere in a region.

Hand-entered

Read in the news and typed in.

What you do with it

It is not a dashboard you watch. It is somewhere you go.

Go straight there

One box that takes whatever you have. A coordinate the way it is actually written — 67.8548, 20.5236, or N 59 19.8 E 018 04.2 off a kneeboard, or degrees-minutes-seconds off a chart. An ICAO code. An airport name. A town. It tells you which of those it decided you meant, because a coordinate that was read and a name looked up in somebody else's gazetteer are different kinds of answer.

Drop to the ground

Fall from orbit to street level in one move, and stand at a spot you pick. Real terrain underneath, real buildings around you.

Walk the street

Google's Street View panorama, embedded and walkable. Follow the arrows down the road and the globe walks with you — turn in the photograph and the map turns too.

Fly into photoreal 3D

Hand the camera to Google's photorealistic mesh at the same coordinate, tilt and heading. Real roofs, real trees, real shadows. Hand it back and you are where you left off, to the metre.

Make it run on a laptop

The globe redraws sixty times a second whether or not anything changed — invisible on a desktop with its own card, a loud fan on a thin laptop. Performance mode makes drawing conditional instead of constant: things that move still animate, fog and atmosphere and 3D buildings come off, terrain asks for half the detail. It changes how often the picture is painted, never what the data says. The app times its own frames and offers the switch, once, if it is drawing too slowly.

Switch the optics

Dark ops chart, satellite, 10 m Sentinel-2, thermal, night vision, FLIR — and a CRT pass with real barrel distortion and scan lines when you want the footage to look like it came off a screen.

Step back through time

Pull the day slider and the imagery goes with it. A burn scar today is a brown patch; the same scar five days back is a story about how it got there.

Measure and mark

A ruler that measures what is actually drawn — the straight line through space, the ground distance and the climb between them. Marks you can save and fly back to.

Turn on detection

Reticles and designators on contacts — SAT-37218, MIL-GAF942, SEA-265068000 — spread across the screen by cell so the labels never pile up.

Go commercial-safe

One switch withdraws every source whose licence is not clean for a monetised video, and says in plain words what it took away and why.

What it costs

It runs on your machine, and most of it needs no account at all.

There is no server but yours, no telemetry and no sign-up. Tested with the key file emptied: 8 163 aircraft, 1 591 cameras, radar, launches, quakes, volcanoes, borders and outbreaks all still arrive. Keys only widen coverage, and every switch that needs one says which and where to get it rather than drawing nothing.

What it will not do

The gaps are on purpose, and each one has a reason.

No Telegram channels. Technically simple and left out: it means republishing war footage from channels with agendas, geoparsed by guesswork onto a map. The honesty the rest of this is built on does not survive that.

No map of exposed devices. A chart of individually located cameras and control systems, on a public stream, is a targeting aid rather than a picture of the world.

No little animated cars. There is no feed of individual vehicles, so they would have to be invented — and this app took out its simulated traffic once already for exactly that.

Built on other people's work

Fifty-odd open sources, each named on screen with its licence: NASA, USGS, OpenStreetMap, Natural Earth, CelesTrak, the Smithsonian, WHO, NOAA, OpenSky, Digitraffic, TeleGeography, Radio Browser, the KiwiSDR volunteers and many more. The app carries the full list, and the commercial-safe switch knows which of them cannot be monetised.

Global Command View · a local app, one Python file and a browser · no account, no telemetry, no server but yours.
