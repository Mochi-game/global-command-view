The radar layer explains itself now — a colour key on screen, and click any

## Download

**GlobalCommandView-1.7.11.zip** below.

Right-click the ZIP before unpacking it, choose Properties, tick **Unblock**,
then extract — that saves you every Windows warning afterwards. Then run
**Install Global Command View.cmd**.

> ### ⚠ Upgrading? Copy two things first
>
> Copy **`keys.json`** and the **`data`** folder somewhere safe before you
> install over an existing copy. That is every key you have set up and every view
> you have saved.
>
> Unpacking over the old folder does not touch them — they are kept out of the
> download on purpose. The ways people lose them are deleting the old folder to
> start clean, or unpacking somewhere new and later tidying away the folder the
> keys were still in. Ten seconds, and the risk is gone. Nothing here is
> recoverable from anyone else.

## The problem

Switch **Radar backscatter** on and the globe turns purple and grey, and nothing
on screen says what any of it is or what to do next. Everything written about
the layer so far was written for somebody who already knew what radar imagery
was — which is nobody switching it on for the first time.

## A key on screen, in words

The legend box bottom right takes the radar layer when it is lit:

| | |
|---|---|
| **dark purple** | water, or anything very smooth |
| **grey** | ordinary ground — fields, forest, bare land |
| **white** | towns, buildings, ships, bridges — anything with a hard edge |

And under it, in one sentence: *radar makes its own light and looks sideways, so
smooth things bounce it away and go dark, and hard edges throw it back and go
bright. It works at night and through cloud.* Plus the date of the picture you
are looking at, and how old it is.

No jargon in any of it. You do not need the word *backscatter* to read the
picture — you need to know that dark means smooth.

## Click any ground and ask when the radar was here

The question a radar picture always raises is how old it is and when a new one
comes. The Copernicus catalogue answers that, and searching it needs no account.

Click the ground with the layer lit, and the card says in plain terms: the
newest picture and how many days ago it was, how often a new one arrives, how
many directions cover that spot, and whether the kind of data that can measure
millimetres of movement was recorded there. Over the Öresund bridge: newest 3
September, a new one about every 1.4 days, five directions, and yes.

**Two repeats, and the card says both.** A new picture arrives from whichever
satellite track flies next — over Sweden that is every day or two. The *same*
viewing angle only comes back every six days, and two pictures only compare with
each other if they were taken from the same angle. Quoting only the friendlier
number would leave you subtracting two images that cannot be subtracted.

## And it tells you where to start

Switching the layer on now says what to do next. The HELP section leads with
three steps — zoom in to about a country, read the key bottom right, click the
ground — before any of the detail.
